## 2. Idle devices: a per-stage ledger, then a scheduler

The owner has seen stretches where the CPU, the GPU or both are idle during a fit, with a thousand columns and about eighty FE families that could be evaluated in parallel. A GPU idle for tens of seconds is a defect to be explained, not a measurement to be accepted.

1. **Per-stage record.** Every MRMR step writes, into the existing sampler timeline (`RSS_TICK_S`, `tick_utilization.py`, `timeline_idle_windows.py`, `hardware_state.py`): the timestamp of its start, the timestamp of its end, the size of its input data in GB, and the device it ran on (cpu or gpu, and which gpu). The sampler already reports CPU percent, GPU percent, free RAM and free VRAM in GB; the stage record is joined to it on time.
2. **The question asked before every stage.** For each stage, is there, in the window before it, a device with low load and enough free memory to hold the stage's current data? If there is, why was the stage not started earlier? The answer goes into a table (stage, window, idle device, free memory, blocking dependency or missing implementation), and every row of the table is either closed by a change or explained by a real data dependency.
3. **Use ALL executors.** The owner rents servers with 128 and more cores and sometimes several GPUs. One saturated GPU is not enough if 127 of 128 cores idle for the whole fit. For every stage the work available at that point (columns, FE families, candidate pairs, permutation batches) is split over all executors (every CPU core group, every GPU) in proportion to what each executor can do: a cost model of seconds per work item per device, measured by the existing KTC machinery and refreshed from the stage records, and a dispatcher that assigns the available items by that model. The goal metric is the one already adopted (utilization as the efficiency measure): CPU percent and GPU percent timelines with the sync counts, next to the wall time, on a 128-core configuration as well as on this machine.
4. Independent FE families run concurrently where their inputs are ready (the stage graph of the fit gives the dependencies); a stage that waits for a device must have a recorded reason.

## 2a. The scheduler is an optimization problem, solved for the machine it runs on (owner requirement, 2026-11-25)

The assignment of stages, and of the divisible work inside a stage, to the CPU and to each GPU must adapt to the hardware of the machine that runs mlframe, and it must be the solution of an operation research problem: not a fixed role per stage and not a rule of thumb. Machines differ in the number and speed of CPU cores and in memory bandwidth; they have one GPU or several; the GPUs differ in VRAM, SM and CUDA core counts, clocks, free memory and bus bandwidth. A plan that is right for a 4 GB card with 8 cores is wrong for a server with 128 cores and two GPUs of different sizes. The planning of the load of the hardware has to be mathematically optimal, with a stated gap to a lower bound.

1. **Calibration (warm-up).** On the first fit on a machine, and whenever its hardware fingerprint changes (CPU model and core count, and for each GPU its name, SM count, total VRAM and driver), MRMR runs a micro-benchmark of a few seconds in total (budget 3 s; skipped when the cache holds the fingerprint). It times representative work items on the CPU and on every GPU separately: pair-MI counting per pair and row, the batched sort and argsort, histogram and binning, the permutation-null kernel, the rank and correlation products, and the host-device transfer bandwidth in both directions, with the free memory read at the same moment. The result is a speed table by kind of work and device and a transfer table, stored by the existing KTC machinery under the fingerprint and refreshed online from the stage records of every fit (an exponential average), so a throttled or shared card is seen by the next plan. Entries that the KTC already holds for the device are read, not measured again (item 6 below).
2. **The model.** The fit is a DAG of stages. Each stage has its work (a function of rows, columns, pairs and permutations: the cost laws already measured for the kernels), the size of its input and output data, the devices that have an implementation of it, and whether it is divisible (columns, pairs, permutation batches, FE families). The planning problem is scheduling on unrelated parallel machines (the CPU core groups and each GPU) with precedence constraints, transfer times between devices that depend on where the data sits, and a memory capacity per device (the working set of a stage must fit in the free VRAM or RAM above the cushion). The objective is the makespan (the wall time); the device utilization is the tie-break. For a divisible stage the share of each device is a variable (a divisible-load model: at the optimum the devices that take part finish together, weighted by their transfer cost).
3. **The solver.** The instance is small (about a hundred stages and a handful of machines), so it is solved to optimality or to a stated gap: a constraint or mixed-integer program (CP-SAT or MILP) for the stage-to-device assignment and the order, a linear program for the shares of the divisible stages, with a time limit well under a second at planning time. A list-scheduling heuristic on the same cost model is the fallback when the solver is not installed or the limit is hit. Every plan carries a lower bound (the longest path of the DAG with each stage on its fastest feasible device, and the total work over the total speed) and the gap to it. The plan is recomputed on the remaining DAG whenever a stage ends with a duration that differs from the model by more than a tolerance (a rolling horizon), so a wrong estimate is corrected inside the fit.
4. **Safety and equivalence.** The schedule may change where a stage runs, never what the fit selects: a stage run on two devices must satisfy the selection-equivalence bar already adopted. With one device, or no GPU, the planner reproduces today's behaviour. A device without enough free memory for a stage is excluded from that stage by the capacity constraint, not found out by a failed attempt. Threads and GPU streams only, no process lanes.
5. **Observability.** Each fit records its plan (stage, device, planned start and end, share), the realized times from the stage ledger, the makespan against the lower bound, and the error of the cost model per stage; the idle-device report compares the planned and the realized timelines.

6. **Item 1a (owner, 2026-11-25): the kernel tuning cache first, the warm-up only for what it lacks.** The speeds and transfer rates of item 1 are read from the KTC store where it already holds a valid entry for the device, and the micro-benchmark measures only the missing entries. The owner is not sure that the KTC is computed for every GPU of unique hardware, so the first task of this section is to verify it and to write the answer into the tracker. What is known from the code now (`pyutilz/performance/kernel_tuning/cache/cache_base.py`, `hw_fingerprint`): the key of an entry is `cpu_<CPU brand string>_gpu_<GPU name>_cc<capability>_d<live device index>_vram<power-of-two GiB class>_drv<driver>_rt<runtime>_nb<numba>_cupy<cupy>_t<numba threads>`; it follows the LIVE CUDA device; `registry._group_gpus_by_model` tunes one device per group of GPUs with the same first word of the name and capability; an entry holds the winning variant per region of its axes (rows, pairs). Not in the key: the CPU clock, the physical core count, the memory speed and bandwidth; the GPU SM count (the provenance of a saved entry records it, the key does not), the memory clock, the power limit and boost state, the exact VRAM (12 GB and 16 GB share the class vram16g). To check, each with a test or a recorded run: (a) two cards that differ only in the SM count, clocks, power limit or a VRAM size inside one class get the same key, so one tuning is applied to both: what does the mismatch cost, and which of these attributes belong in the key and which in a separate profile; (b) on a box with two different GPUs every device really has its own entries (the key follows the live device, so the tuning must be run with each device current, and a fit that routes work to device 1 must read device 1's entries, not those of device 0); (c) whether an entry stores cardinal times (seconds per work item for each variant, which the planner needs) or only the winner (enough to choose a variant on a device, not enough to balance work between devices); (d) the CPU side: the model string is in the key but the clock, the physical cores and the memory bandwidth are not, so two machines with the same CPU and different RAM speed or power plan share an entry; (e) nothing in the KTC covers the host-device transfer bandwidth, the free-memory state or the thermal state of a device, and the stage records refresh those; (f) the age of an entry against the driver and the thermal state it was measured in. The owner's decision (2026-11-25): the missing hardware attributes are a defect of pyutilz and are fixed there. Changing the key forces a re-tune of every kernel on every host that has a cache, so the attributes are added as a hardware profile (static attributes plus measured memory and transfer bandwidths, cached by identity, a few seconds at most) that is stored with the entries and read by the planner; which of them also enter the key is decided from the cost measured in (a) and (d). The result is a table (what the KTC gives, what the warm-up must add, what the stage records refresh) and the changes to the KTC key and the entry format that the planner needs; every gap is closed by a change or recorded as the reason for the warm-up item.

The baseline to replace or wrap is the stage-concurrency scheduler (`_fe_stage_concurrency`, `_fe_device_scheduler`: lanes, slot pools, fixed device roles), which is a heuristic today.
